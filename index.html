About

Researcher, Analyst & Educator

Timothy D. Spivey is a doctoral candidate in Business Administration, with a concentration in Business Analytics, at the University of South Alabama, where his dissertation examines what mandatory breach disclosure timing actually does — to markets, to organizations, and to boards.

The question is not whether disclosure matters. It is whether mandating when a firm discloses produces the benefit regulators assumed it would, and whether the public data used to answer that question can support the answer. The empirical design is a natural experiment built on the FCC's 2007 mandatory disclosure rule (47 CFR § 64.2011), applied to 1,054 public breach records from 2006 to 2025 that resolve to 489 distinct breach events.

His broader work follows two lines. One examines security culture: how fear-based policies, rigid IT controls and workplace surveillance can create the very risks they are meant to prevent. The other extends into telecom markets: how competition from fiber and fixed wireless shapes cable incumbents’ service and networks, and how operators should decide which customers to retain.

Outside the dissertation, he supervises management information systems at Mediacom Communications and teaches Introduction to Entrepreneurship and Entrepreneurship Marketing and Sales as an adjunct at Pensacola State College. Nearly twenty years of telecom operations lies beneath the research: he has built the reporting pipelines, sat in the escalation calls, and watched compliance decisions get made under real pressure. That is why the empirical questions he chases are the ones practitioners recognize.

He holds an MBA in Business Analytics from the University of West Florida and a BAS in Organizational Administration from Pensacola State College — where he now teaches.

Essay 1 Job market paper

Records Are Not Events: Disclosure Timing, Regulatory Status, and Market Reactions to Data Breaches

Public breach databases record notifications, not incidents: one breach can appear many times, filing dates can stand in for breach dates, and industry codes stand in for regulatory status. This essay rebuilds the data at the event level, takes regulatory status from the FCC's Form 499 registry, and uses the FCC's 2007 disclosure rule as a natural experiment to show how those measurement choices shape estimated market reactions to breaches.
