A public-data retention playbook for broadband

Who leaves, who to save, and what it costs

Most churn analysis stops at a risk score. This piece follows the decision all the way through, from who is likely to leave to whom it pays to contact. It rebuilds the standard analysis on four public datasets, fixes the places where the usual charts mislead, and shows what changes when every probability is calibrated and every list is judged by profit.

Why public and partly fictional data? No operator publishes customer-level records, for privacy and legal reasons. So the methods are shown on a standard public teaching dataset, and the main findings are checked against real operator data or a randomized experiment wherever public data allow.     Cable broadband has a connect problem, and retention is the lever it controls

    In the third quarter of 2026 the two largest US cable operators lost broadband customers while AT&T added customers on fiber and fixed wireless.

    Source: ACSI Telecommunications Study 2026, American Customer Satisfaction Index LLC (released May 19, 2026)5. Source: JD Power 2026 U.S. Internet Service Provider Satisfaction Study (released Oct. 8, 2026); the wireless–wired gap narrowed by 8 points from 20256. Cited for context only.

    Losses at this scale usually get read as a churn problem. Charter's management described it differently. Its legacy business (before the Cox acquisition) lost 156,000 internet customers, against 109,000 a year earlier, "driven by lower connects year over year, while the churn rate was essentially flat"3.

    Net change equals connects minus disconnects. When new connects fall, the base shrinks even if the same share of customers leaves each month. A retention program can't fix an acquisition shortfall. It can make each remaining customer last longer, which is worth more when replacing them gets harder.

    For scale, real monthly churn is small. AT&T reported 0.92% postpaid phone churn for the quarter4, and the real wireless carrier data used below had monthly churn of about 1.8%, as documented by the researchers who released it20. Keep that range in mind for chapter 3.

    3

    When they leave, and why the usual survival chart misleads here

    One of the most widely used churn datasets can't support the survival curve it is usually shown with. Reading it correctly changes the story.

    The IBM sample is a snapshot. IBM's documentation describes its churn flag two ways: customers who "left within the last month" and customers who "left this quarter." Either way, it records departures over one short window, and customers who left before that window are not in the data at all. A Kaplan–Meier curve treats every current customer's tenure as an observed history and counts only one window of departures against it, so it understates risk badly.

    The correct reading is a hazard: of the customers at tenure month t, what share left during the window? Chain those hazards together and you get the survival curve the data actually imply, under either reading.

    This limitation has been pointed out before21, but most tutorials built on this dataset still draw the standard curve. What follows measures how large the error is.

    Churn hazard by tenure (smoothed), with banded rates
    Share still a customer after 12 months
    ContractUsual chart (Kaplan–Meier)If churn = last monthIf churn = last quarter

    The usual chart says most month-to-month customers stay a year. Read correctly, the data say fewer than one in ten would. 26.5% churn in one window is about 9.8% a month even on the quarterly reading, far above the roughly 1–2% real operators report. So the sample's churn levels are fictional. What survives is the shape: risk is highest in the first months and falls with tenure for month-to-month customers, while one-year contract customers show no early spike.

    What a customer is worth depends on how you model retention

    Two models that agree on first-year retention can disagree by a quarter on lifetime value.

    Share of a new cohort still active, by month
    Mixed customers (shifted-beta-geometric)Constant monthly retention

    The common lifetime-value formula assumes every customer has the same monthly retention rate15. Real customer bases are a mix of people likely to leave soon and people who will stay for years. As the restless ones leave, the group that remains is stickier, so aggregate retention rises with tenure even if no individual changes. The shifted-beta-geometric model captures this with two parameters14.

    This chapter is an illustration with stated assumptions, not an estimate from data. The public datasets don't include a real cohort followed over time, which is what this model needs.

    Who to contact: calibrate first, then judge models by profit

    A model's ranking skill is not the decision. The decision needs honest probabilities and an explicit profit rule.

    Model performance on three datasets (out-of-fold)
    Dataset · modelAUCBrierCalib. error

    AUC measures ranking. Calibration error measures whether a 30% prediction means 30%; the Brier score combines calibration and ranking (lower is better for both). Calibration is what lets a probability drive a decision16. On the IBM sample, plain logistic regression matches gradient boosting.

    Cell2Cell US wireless data20, 50/50 sample. Prior correction: p′ = p·(π/ρ) ÷ [p·(π/ρ) + (1−p)·(1−π)/(1−ρ)], with true base rate π ≈ 1.8% and sample rate ρ ≈ 50%. Profit is reweighted to the real population. Contact rule: offer when p × acceptance × (value − offer) > (1 − p) × offer + contact cost. At the defaults that means p must exceed 11 ÷ 67 ≈ 16.4%.

    Uncorrected probabilities would contact everyone and lose money. With realistic probabilities, a model this weak (AUC ) only pays when the offer is nearly free. That is a finding, not a failure: it tells you to invest in better signals before a bigger campaign.

    Who to save: risk is not the same as responsiveness

    The customers most likely to leave are not always the ones an offer changes. That takes a randomized test to see.

    Field experiments in retention have found that targeting by predicted risk can be ineffective, because the riskiest customers are not necessarily the most persuadable7. Some outreach backfires: in one mobile carrier experiment, customers sent a well-meant plan recommendation churned at 10% over three months, against 6% in the untouched control group8. Uplift models target the change an offer causes, not the risk itself12.

    No public telecom dataset includes a randomized retention offer. So this chapter uses a real randomized experiment from retail email (64,000 customers, a third receiving no email)19 to show the method. It demonstrates the technique, not a telecom effect size.

    Extra visits per 1,000 customers in the base if the targeted 30% were emailed. Average effect = visit rate with email minus without. Interval: bootstrap over customers, model scores held fixed.

    Targeting helped most where the email's effect varied across customers. Which targeting method won was too close to call. For the women's email, the effect varied a lot across customers: the top three deciles gained about three times as much as the bottom half, and both models' 30% lists gained more visits than a random 30% would. Uplift targeting was ahead for the women's email and behind for the other two, but every gap's 95% interval includes zero. The practical rule: hold out a random control group in every retention campaign, large enough to tell these apart.

    Price locks: the break-even rule

    A price guarantee pays only if it cuts churn by more than it cuts revenue per customer.

    A price guarantee trades revenue per customer for longer customer lives. The arithmetic is simple. In steady state, the customer base equals new connects divided by the churn rate, so revenue moves with price ÷ churn.

    A lock that gives up 5% of revenue per customer must cut churn by at least 5% in relative terms (say, from 1.50% to 1.43% a month) just to break even. The discount goes to every customer who takes the lock, including those who would have stayed at full price anyway, which is why the bar is that high. Any lift in new connects is extra.

    Steady-state comparison with connects held constant. Measured on contribution margin instead of revenue, the bar is higher (a 5% price cut takes a bigger share of margin), so treat the revenue rule as a lower bound and a first screen, not a forecast.

    Using this responsibly

    Removing sensitive attributes from a model changed who gets contacted only modestly. Audit who gets contacted, not just what goes into the model.

    Who a top-20% contact list reaches (IBM sample)
    GroupChurn rateContacted, full modelContacted, attributes removedContacted but would have stayed

    "Would have stayed" = share of non-churners in the group who are contacted (false-positive rate, full model). Attributes removed: gender, senior status, partner, dependents. Model AUC .

    Seniors are contacted at more than twice the rate of other customers, and removing senior status and three other attributes only modestly changes that (38% to 34%, against 16% to 17%). Other features correlated with age still shape the list. Seniors in this sample also churn more (42% vs 24%), so much of the gap is accurate targeting. Non-churning seniors are contacted nearly three times as often as other non-churners, though. The question for the business is whether the offers they receive help them.

    Three practices follow:

    • Keep sensitive attributes out of targeting rules, but keep them for auditing.
    • Report contact rates and error rates by group before every launch.
    • Watch who pays for retention offers. Loyal, low-risk customers who never receive discounts end up subsidizing those who do.
    What monitoring looks like in production
    Calibration drift

    Compare predicted and actual churn by score band every month. Recalibrate when they separate.

    Population shift

    Track the mix of tenure, plans and payment methods against the training data (for example, a population stability index).

    Holdout lift

    Keep a random untreated group in every campaign and report the difference, not the save count.

    Data, methods and licenses

    Everything here runs from public data in Python. Every number on this page is generated by one script, which is published with the page.

    Why fictional data, and why it is still useful

    Real customer records can't be published. Operators don't release account-level data. Customer information is confidential, and US rules on customer proprietary network information limit how carriers may use and disclose it22. An honest public piece can't use an operator's own records, and this one uses none.

    The IBM sample is a shared benchmark. It is one of the most widely used public telecom churn datasets, published by IBM as sample data, with nearly complete fields (11 blank charge values, all for brand-new customers). Anyone can rerun every number here and compare it with other work.

    It is used only where fiction can't mislead. It demonstrates methods and relative patterns (which segments churn more, how a calibrated model compares with an uncalibrated one). No conclusion about real churn levels rests on it, and chapter 3 shows why its levels can't be taken literally.

    Real data check the claims that matter most. Segment patterns and model performance are retested on a real operator's records. Calibration and profit are tested on a real US carrier. Targeting is tested on a real randomized experiment, from another industry. Two parts rest on the IBM sample alone, the churn reasons (ch. 4) and the fairness audit (ch. 9), because no public real dataset has reasons or demographic fields; read those as demonstrations of the method.

    Models

    scikit-learn logistic regression and histogram gradient boosting. All performance figures are out-of-fold from 5-fold stratified cross-validation, seed 42. Intervals on rates are 95% Wilson intervals.

    Profit

    Expected profit per customer = churners reached × (save rate × (value − offer) − contact cost) − non-churners reached × (offer + contact cost). Expected maximum profit averages the best achievable profit over a Beta(6, 14) save rate10.

    Uplift

    T-learner with gradient boosting13. Qini values count treated responders minus scaled control responders among the top-ranked, divided by the treated share so they read as "if every targeted customer were emailed." They are not normalized to a perfect model.

    Limitations. The IBM sample is fictional and its churn levels are not realistic. The real datasets are older, small, or from other industries, and the uplift experiment is retail email. The lifetime-value chapter uses assumed parameters. Complaints and other predictors are associations, not causes. Voluntary and involuntary churn are not separated, and promotion end dates are not in any public dataset. None of this describes any particular company. The point is the method, and every step can be rerun on an operator's own data.
